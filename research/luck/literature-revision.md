# Literature revision: reference classes and philosophical accounts of luck

Prepared October 5, 2026 for the revision of Part I. This note distinguishes
primary texts actually inspected from excerpts and abstracts. The suggested
prose below is a selection for integration, not an instruction to insert every
author or to enlarge the essay indiscriminately.

## Sources and specific findings

### John Venn: the individual belongs to several classes

**Read:** *The Logic of Chance*, third edition (1888), Chapter IX, especially
§§12–13 and 19, printed pp.213–220. [Original text, Gutenberg
transcription](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/57359/pg57359-images.html).
The book first appeared in 1866; this reading is of the 1888 edition.

Venn's John Smith example shows that one person can belong to intersecting
classes with different survival frequencies. His discussion also recognizes
the practical limit of increasingly specific classes: adequate numbers of
instances may disappear. Do not describe the reference-class problem as a
discovery of this series.

### Hans Reichenbach: a primary excerpt, not a claimed full-book reading

**Consulted:** the indexed primary excerpt on printed p.18 of *Philosophical
Foundations of Probability* (1949), Proceedings of the Berkeley Symposium,
pp.1–20. [Berkeley library's original
PDF](https://digicoll.lib.berkeley.edu/nanna/record/112790/files/math_s1_article-01.pdf?registerDownload=1&version=1&withMetadata=0&withWatermark=0).
The indexed excerpt is readable through search; direct requests returned an
empty 202 response, so this is **not** a claim to have inspected the full paper.
The separately published *The Theory of Probability* (1949), §72, was not read.

The excerpt proposes the narrowest reference class with reliable statistics.
It supplies historical context, not an automatic solution for modern
prediction. Prefer the fuller critical argument in Hájek for the essay's main
reference. If the book is cited, state that Hájek discusses its proposal; do not
silently substitute a secondhand quotation for direct reading of the book.

### Alan Hájek: explicit conditioning does not choose the right condition

**Read:** “The reference class problem is your problem too,” *Synthese* 156
(2007), 563–585: §1, §2.1.3, and §3.4. [Full published
paper](https://www.fitelson.org/probability/hajek_rc.pdf);
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-006-9138-5).

On pp.568–569, Hájek challenges the narrowest-class prescription: reliability
needs justification and candidate classes need not be nested. On pp.583–584,
his proposed primacy of conditional probabilities leaves the epistemological
question of which condition should guide a decision unresolved. He argues
that variants of the problem extend beyond frequentism. These are his
arguments, not a demonstrated consensus across interpretations.

### Duncan Pritchard: modal closeness differs from probabilistic likelihood

**Read:** “The Modal Account of Luck,” *Metaphilosophy* 45 (2014), 594–619,
accepted manuscript §§1–3 and 5. [Author's manuscript, UC
repository](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4560725q/qt4560725q.pdf);
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/meta.12103).

Manuscript pp.5–6 examine how easily an event could have failed under nearby
possibilities with relevant initial conditions fixed. This modal closeness is
not a probability. Pp.9–10 separate value and significance from his account of
lucky events; pp.16–18 reject lack of control as sufficient. His refined 2014
view should not be replaced by a quotation of his older 2005 formulation.

### Wayne Riggs: a competing control account remains contested

**Read:** publisher's abstract only, “Luck, Knowledge, and Control,” in
*Epistemic Value* (2009), 204–222. [Oxford
abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/32937/chapter-abstract/278522547);
[DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199231188.003.0010).

Riggs defends a lack-of-control account against Pritchard's modal approach.
This establishes a live disagreement. It does not warrant reconstructing
Riggs's complete conditions or adjudicating the dispute without reading the
chapter. No philosophical consensus on a complete definition is asserted.

## Proposed English passages

### After the binomial illustration: locate the existing problem

This difficulty has a history. In *The Logic of Chance*, John Venn asks which
of the several classes containing one person should supply a prediction.
Making the class increasingly specific creates another difficulty: there may
be too few comparable cases left. Reichenbach later proposed using the
narrowest class with reliable statistics. [Hájek's
analysis](https://www.fitelson.org/probability/hajek_rc.pdf) shows why that
prescription leaves substantive choices: classes can overlap without one
containing another, and reliability itself needs an argument. Declaring a
reference before the outcome prevents hindsight from rewriting it. Whether
that reference should guide the decision remains a question about evidence
and the purpose of the prediction.

### Near the working definition: distinguish the proposed measurement

There are also competing philosophical accounts of luck. [Pritchard's modal
account](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4560725q/qt4560725q.pdf) asks how
easily an event could have failed, with relevant initial conditions held
fixed. A nearby alternative is defined by similarity, rather than by its
probability. Control accounts instead emphasize the agent's relation to the
event; [Riggs defends that
approach](https://academic.oup.com/book/32937/chapter-abstract/278522547)
against the modal account. The predictive excess below addresses a narrower
question: how favorable was this realization under our declared forecast?
To investigate fragility, I would also ask which credible changes could
reverse the result. To investigate agency, I would ask which changes the
actor could actually make. These inquiries can inform each other while
retaining their distinct evidence requirements.

### A bridge to the AI example, in the author's own voice

For an AI evaluation, those questions become concrete. A score can exceed
the baseline on a sampled set of prompts. Its advantage may disappear when
the same task is phrased differently, or survive those changes but depend
on access to a resource unavailable to the comparison system. The first
observation concerns a predictive reference; the second concerns specified
perturbations; the third concerns opportunity and the comparison's design.
To call the result lucky before examining these distinctions is to let one
word hide several unfinished investigations.

## Integration recommendations

1. Introduce the reference-class problem by name and retain the historical
   acknowledgment, but let the fifteen-out-of-twenty example carry the argument.
   Hájek is the substantive source for why choosing in advance is insufficient.
2. Keep the working quantity named **signed predictive excess**. A prospective
   reference disciplines a descriptive measurement; it does not define all luck,
   justify the reference, or establish a causal attribution.
3. Add modal fragility as a question rather than a number derived from a tail.
   A perturbation test requires an explicit set of allowable changes. Its
   empirical failure frequency requires an additional sampling distribution.
   Counting imagined alternatives supplies neither automatically.
4. Continue using a declared utility to distinguish favorable and unfavorable
   outcomes. That is the series' applied decision question, not a component
   attributed to Pritchard's 2014 metaphysical definition.
5. Keep Knight for the warrant of probability models and Nagel for the ethical
   boundary. The new sources address reference selection and the nature of luck;
   they perform different work and should not be written as a single lineage
   unanimously endorsing this series' framework.
6. Avoid a concluding pile of exclusions. State the contribution positively:
   specify the prospective comparison, examine robustness to credible changes,
   document agency, and then decide which claim the evidence supports.

## Effect on the original claims

The numerical demonstrations remain valid under their stated models. The
important revision is interpretive: the existing formulation makes advance
declaration sound close to sufficient justification and uses a descriptive
excess as a broad operational definition. The proposed text places that excess
within a more explicit program of inquiry. It recognizes existing scholarship
and adds a concrete robustness question without pretending that a short
literature section has solved either philosophical dispute.

## Final independent review of the revised Parts I and III
